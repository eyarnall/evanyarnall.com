Better ways to measure infant movement

Comparing video coding, wearable sensors and markerless tracking, plus the coding pipelines behind them.

Early signs of cerebral palsy often show up in how infants move, but measuring that movement reliably is hard. Our lab compares several ways of capturing it:

  • Video observation, scored through behavioral coding
  • Wearable sensors (inertial measurement units, or IMUs)
  • Markerless motion tracking with DeepLabCut during infant reaching

I build and maintain the lab’s behavioral coding pipelines in Datavyu, using Ruby and Python, including the inter-rater reliability workflows that make the coded data trustworthy.

What behavioral coding looks like

Coding means watching video and marking exactly when a behavior happens, such as each moment an infant’s hand touches a toy. Two trained coders score the same sessions, and their agreement (inter-rater reliability) tells us whether the codes can be trusted. Try it:

Coding which hand

Real coding schemes go further than “touching or not.” To study how infants learn to use their two hands together, we also code which hand is on the toy: the left, the right, or both. Telling these apart reliably is the first step toward spotting more advanced skills, like pulling apart the pieces of a toy, opening containers, or putting things into them.

Video or wearable sensors?

Video coding is detailed but slow: someone has to watch every second. Wearable sensors (IMUs), worn on the wrists and ankles like bracelets, record movement automatically, but their continuous signal has to be turned into “moving” and “not moving” with a threshold.
